## Who he is (as it bears on the advice)

Founder of **Game Dev Underground (GDU)** — a YouTube channel and community "built, finished, and launched better games." A recovering serial quitter and perfectionist: **~100 projects started, ~18–19 finished**; 10–15 years making games (an engine, a level editor, lots of prototypes) with **nothing published until 2015**. His in-progress game is **_Philophobia_**, a platformer about fear where the screen is blacked out around the player. His entire body of work is about the **psychology and discipline of actually finishing** — which is precisely the coaching doc's #1 blocker ([career-coach.md](career-coach.md)): disorganization → can't see the next step → blocked → motivation cliff, plus the perfectionism spiral.

## Core ideas

### 1. Finishing is the whole game
~90%+ of games never ship. "Quitting is just so easy" — so **just finishing a game puts you in the top 10%.** He's completed ~18–19 of ~100+ starts, and couldn't have finished those without deciding the rest were dead.

### 2. Over-scoping is the root cause of most quitting
Most "technical obstructions" aren't a skill problem — they're a **scope** problem (a multiplayer RTS as a first project). Rule: **"cut it in half"** — however small you think your game is, cut it in half. Avoid **multiplayer** and **animation-heavy 3D** when starting. Think in **scope of work**: 1,000 hand-drawn weapons is impossible; 1,000 text-defined weapons is trivial.

### 3. The longer/bigger the project, the higher the odds you quit
It's just probability. **Start small.** Game jams (his are Friday–Monday) let you run the whole pipeline to a finish, with "no time for the boring work." Plan for the fact that a real project *has* a boring middle.

### 4. The Wundt curve — enjoyment vs. familiarity
Not familiar at all = not enjoyable; totally familiar = not enjoyable; a **spike of enjoyment in the middle**. As you work a game you get more familiar → less enjoyment, both building it *and* playing it. The trap: devs get bored, assume players are bored too, and pile on features (he once turned Philophobia into a roguelike; his mastermind group said "Tim, what are you doing?"). **Don't exploit your own boredom by adding stuff.** Sometimes he restarts a project because a fresh start is genuinely more enjoyable.

### 5. Discipline over motivation (and knowing when to stop)
"It's not so much about motivation, it's about discipline. You've got to know how to work even when you don't feel like working — **but also know when to stop working even when you do feel like working**," or you burn out. Reference: **_The War of Art_ (Steven Pressfield)** and its personified **"Resistance"** — a real force pulling you from creating every day that **grows the deeper into a project you are**; you wake up and fight it daily.

### 6. "I don't have time" is a priority problem, not a time problem
Everyone has the same 24 hours as Elon Musk / Bill Gates / Gary Vaynerchuk. People "have no time" but binge Netflix for four hours. Game dev is, for him, "not the best use of my time" financially — he makes it a priority anyway. **Schedule it**: daily if possible (keeps the habit, always making progress), else 2–3×/week. Daily is his preference. "If you don't make time, it's probably because you don't want to."

### 7. Whether to push through depends on your goal
If the goal is **fun** (hobby), quitting when it stops being fun is fine. If the goal is a **commercial release**, you must power through the non-fun work — controls, subtitles, localization. "If you're not prepared to do the hard stuff, it's gonna be really hard making the game."

### 8. Comparison kills projects
His first game was going to be a solo Blender remake of **Halo 1** — "setting myself up for failure." Playing one great game (Hollow Knight) makes you look at yours and quit. Mantra: **"the only person you should compare yourself to is the person you were yesterday."** Better still: **don't put yourself in a position where you *have* to compare** — a genuinely different subject or twist (Philophobia's personification of fear) means you're not held to the same yardstick.

### 9. The negativity bias — one comment shouldn't move you
26,000 positive people, one "your game sucks," and you believe the one. Brains **amplify negatives on purpose** — a survival instinct (the tiger in the woods outranks the happy crowd); with basic needs met, social rejection gets over-weighted. Never rebuild your game around one negative comment. Extract the useful signal, decide for yourself if it's legit, and **"don't let them dictate whether you finish your game."**

### 10. Feedback: the middle path
New devs take **all** the ideas (→ over-scope) or **none** (→ arrogance). The answer is in between: *does this fit my vision?* Picking what goes in and what stays out **is the job**. An audience hands you great ideas that are dangerous when you're trying to scope down.

### 11. Shiny-object syndrome — write every idea down
He keeps a years-old list of game ideas. Writing one down **releases it** (it's out of your head, stops nagging) **and** lets you judge it later without the emotional attachment. "More than half the ideas I write down that I thought were brilliant are just… why would I write this down?"

### 12. Running multiple projects is over-scoping in disguise
Starting "just a little side project" → you like the new idea better → now you have two. His fix: **game jams** scratch the new-thing itch, boxed to a weekend, "and I tell myself I'm not touching it after that." Also **"Finish Friday"** — sit down live on stream and either **complete** the project or **delete** it off the hard drive. The stakes trained him to finish.

### 13. Experiment before you commit; quantity over quality while learning
Jumping into one idea too fast → better ideas surface later → wasted work. Do several jams back-to-back, then **judge by what you actually completed** and pick one to expand. "Finding the quality in the quantity is a lot easier than designing the quality from the get-go." (Same as his content model: post daily, email only the best.)

### 14. Don't pause a project — quit it or commit to it
His **#1 reason for quitting**: "I tried to pause development" and never came back. A paused project is a **zombie / undead project** — technically dead but still eating mental bandwidth every time you see the folder. Make the call: dead, or active. **"Indecision is almost always worse than the wrong decision."** He explicitly quit ~80 projects *permanently* so he could finish ~18.

### 15. If you take a break, make it a real one with a hard return date
Not an "escape or excuse." He took a conscious month off Philophobia (a cruise to Mexico + a recoup week) with a **specific return day known the whole time** — and came back *looking forward to it*, "which hadn't happened in a long time."

### 16. Losing your work makes you quit — back up redundantly
**Dropbox** (archives every saved version of every file — has saved him from overwrites "so many times"), **plus** a hard drive, **plus** more than one copy. "This is just zeros and ones on a magnetic disk." He lost ~15 years of old projects when a drive died with no second copy.

### 17. "I don't know how to continue" → get outside perspective
Show someone the game; ideally **watch them play, in the same room**, rather than asking "what should I do?" Watch where everyone instinctively double-taps jump or tries to fly — that's a design signal. **Email successful devs** — they have inboxes and often help. And **play games similar in scope** for ideas: he played Hollow Knight, Spelunky, Celeste, Cuphead — the Hollow-Knight-style ability progression is where Philophobia's core "heart mechanic" came from.

### 18. Momentum — the space-shuttle analogy
A shuttle burns **99% of its fuel escaping the atmosphere**; after that it's smooth. Breaking your pattern / inertia takes an enormous boost; then it coasts. Three timescales:
- **Daily momentum** — a habit he's cultivated; *not* his struggle.
- **Life momentum** — built over years; you get faster at engaging *and disengaging*.
- **The 3–9-month-to-a-year project momentum** — "right around how long it takes to make a damn indie game" — the one he **still** struggles with.
~**80% of game studios never make a second game.** The "multi-game momentum" is what matters, and burning out on game one is fatal to it. (Famous authors have ~40 books, not one; Super Meat Boy wasn't McMillen's first game.) **Solo, momentum is far harder — you need a support system.**

### 19. Rock-bottom pressure changes the mindset (the Fifi story)
Broke, choosing between feeding himself and his dog **Fifi**; wanting to jam the gas pedal of his 1986 Camaro but unable to afford the gas. That night his focus changed — he put everything into a business so he'd "never have to do that again." Revised goal after the struggle: not "be a millionaire" but **"go to the grocery store without checking my balance first."** And it wasn't the last time — he's stubborn and keeps re-entering that spot, but solves it a little better each time.

### 20. Overwhelm — the 5 steps
1. **Breathe.** Get alone, give your brain space.
2. **Define specifically what you're trying to achieve.** The more specific, the less ambiguous the next steps.
3. **Break it into real action steps and organize by impact.** "Finish the game engine" is **not** an action item — it has 1,000 shifting sub-pieces. Break down the *most important* piece; order by what would most move the whole / the experience / a viewer's impression (art usually scores high because people judge visually; mechanics first, then art).
4. **Make a concrete per-piece list** — "physics on initial jump," "gravity," "double jump at the apex"; specific sprites/models.
5. **Pick the top thing and go.** Don't deliberate about which is more "efficient" — that's "efficiency garbage." **"Getting started is the most important thing you can do."**

### 21. Perfectionism = obsession = a form of laziness
Obsessive since childhood (a song on repeat for 10 days; locking himself in a room). Perfectionism is what stopped him being a finisher — **10–15 years, an engine, a level editor, countless prototypes, zero published games until 2015**, always because "it's not good enough." "Working on something over and over until it's perfect **is a form of laziness** — you spend 10 days to make it 1% better when you could've shipped at 99% and nobody could tell."

### 22. "Done is better than perfect"
He rejects the Miyamoto line ("a delayed game is eventually good, a rushed game is forever bad") — "not true anymore; you can iterate, patch, DLC, update." If perfectionism is blocking you from publishing, **lower your quality bar** and learn to push things out before they feel ready. (Not: ship garbage.)

### 23. The 90% rule
If something's at **90%, it's good enough — move on.** The last few percent is diminishing returns; those two days are better spent on another critical system.

### 24. Separate creation from editing
**Writing days vs. editing days** — on writing days, editing is *not allowed*. Took his book from stuck-at-one-page-in-6-8-hours to **10–20 pages per 48 hours** (~150 pages total). For YouTube: **one take, no second takes, no editing, push it out** — because he *knows* he'll obsess forever otherwise. He accepts the embarrassing stuff (banging the desk, something on his shirt, rambling) as the price of a video a day for 10 months straight. His early 8-hour whiteboard-animated videos were his **lowest**-performing.

### 25. Volume as the cure for perfectionism
Philophobia: **5 levels a day**, regardless of mood. He hates 50–60% of his own levels and ships them all to testers anyway — the ones he hates are sometimes the ones testers love. Then: let them sit a couple days → **one** design pass → out to testers → **one more** pass after watching a tester video. A hard cap on passes because "I know I'll tweak till infinity." Volume "changes your mindset from someone that obsesses to someone that finishes stuff" — good for self-identity.

### 26. Artificial urgency — Pomodoro and hard stops
A timer (30–60 min) forces prioritization; you can't obsess over one piece. He schedules the hour **right before he has to leave** for a meeting so the urgency is real — "the game-jam mindset: you've got to publish, you don't have time to obsess."

### 27. The perfectionist ↔ corner-cutter spectrum — aim for the middle, learn it from your opposite
**Corner-cutters** ship a lot but miss details and push before ready (and sometimes corner-cut from wanting the easy way → shiny-object hopping). **Perfectionists** never finish, obsess over what doesn't matter, spend years on something that may not sell. He *was* a hard corner-cutter; his LA mentor **Kelly** — an extreme-perfectionist VP who sent thousand-item bullet-point emails about moving an image two inches — rubbed off on him over 2–3 years of building websites together (American Theatre Channel, etc.), and vice versa. "I don't think I could've learned attention to detail from a book." That blend is why he can ship (**22 games in two years**) *and* notice what others miss. **Go hang out with people on the opposite end of your spectrum.**

### 28. Corner-cutting is *correct* for prototypes, first games, and just publishing
"10 days for a 1% improvement" is not worth it. At those stages perfectionism is "an extreme barrier."

### 29. Beware the sunk-cost fallacy (he quit game school 6 months from graduation)
$50k in loans, half a year from a gaming-simulation-programming degree, he quit — felt he had more games experience than his teachers, works for himself, "no one has ever once asked me for a degree; I have experience." He names the **sunk-cost fallacy** directly (why gambling works — chasing losses): "I've spent $50k over 3.5 years, I might as well do six more months" is the trap. He also just likes breaking the pattern; school is "an assembly line" making everyone the same. His mission for GDU: build **"the educational experience for game development that should exist"** — motivation, scope creep, the psychological stuff *and* the technical training — "the thing 18-year-old me could have joined, with none of the [BS] and none of the $400 textbooks."

### 30. Public progress = accountability that carries you through Resistance
People asking "where's the last livestream? what happened to the game?" is real motivation, as is "oh, this is cool." Downside: an audience floods you with good ideas that wreck a scope-down.

### 31. A team is a marriage — set expectations up front
Most team break-ups are miscommunication, shiny-object syndrome, or the wrong teammate. Match work ethic, communication, willingness to talk things out. Agree **before you start** on "here's what happens if you feel like quitting." He states concrete asks — "I need 2 pieces of work / 4 hours a week / 4 videos this month — can you commit? If not, no judgment, but you're not right for this." Upfront agreement removes the guilt of chasing people. Scary for introverts; still better than not doing it. And **speak up early** when you're unhappy — he's guilty of speaking up "way too late."

### 32. "The world needs more indie games"
They have a meditative, artistic effect on people's lives; studios and people burning out or losing momentum is a real loss. The idealistic note under all the tactics.

## Stances (where he plants a flag)

- **Finishing beats everything** — a finished mediocre game beats an unfinished great one.
- **Start small; cut it in half; avoid multiplayer and 3D-animation-heavy first projects.**
- **The longer the project, the higher the quit probability** — plan for the boring middle.
- **Discipline over motivation** — and know when to *stop*, because burnout is real.
- **"No time" is almost always "not a priority"** — schedule it, daily if you can.
- **Whether to push through depends on your goal** (hobby vs. commercial).
- **Never rebuild your game around one negative comment**; extract signal, decide yourself.
- **Taking all feedback OR none are both wrong** — filter by your vision.
- **Write every shiny idea down** — it releases the itch and de-emotionalizes later judgment.
- **Don't run multiple serious projects**; use jams for novelty.
- **Don't pause — decide.** Indecision costs more than a wrong decision.
- **Breaks are good but need a hard return date** and must not be escapes.
- **Back up redundantly** (Dropbox + drive + copies).
- **Stuck? Watch someone play in person; email devs; play same-scope games.**
- **Momentum needs a huge boost to start, then coasts** — solo devs need a support system.
- **Beat overwhelm with the 5 steps, then just start the top item** and ignore "efficiency."
- **Perfectionism is obsession is laziness**; "done is better than perfect"; the 90% rule.
- **Separate creating from editing**; ship in one pass where you can.
- **Use volume** (5 levels/day, a video a day) to turn an obsessor into a finisher.
- **Use timers / artificial deadlines** to force prioritization.
- **Aim for the middle of the perfectionist–corner-cutter spectrum; learn from your opposite.**
- **Corner-cutting is correct for prototypes and first games.**
- **Beware sunk-cost fallacy** (school, dead projects).
- **Team up like a marriage** — vet work ethic, set explicit expectations up front, speak up early.
- **Quantity over quality while learning.**
- **The world needs more indie games; game-dev education is broken.**

## How this applies to the flagship project & pivot

_(Bridge back to [career-coach.md](career-coach.md); see also [[tim-cain]] and [[jonas-tyroller]].)_

- **He is the direct match for the coaching doc's #1 blocker.** The named loop — disorganization → can't see the next step → blocked → motivation cliff — is his entire subject. The **5-step overwhelm process (#20)** is the operational fix: define the specific outcome, break it into real action steps ordered by impact, **pick the top one and start**, ignore "which is more efficient."
- **"Don't pause — quit or commit" (#14)** and the **zombie-project** framing speak straight to a shrinking-runway solo dev with one long project: the danger isn't a wrong call, it's the un-made call. Pair with **momentum (#18)** — the flagline is exactly the "3–9-month-to-a-year" momentum he says is the hardest, and solo makes it harder, so **build a support system** (this also serves the coaching doc's network-rebuild goal).
- **Perfectionism = laziness / "done is better than perfect" / the 90% rule (#21–23)** is the bluntest counter to the "every line must be a reusable foundation" reflex. His **volume method (#25)** — ship 5 units a day, hate half of them, one design pass, cap the passes — is a concrete anti-spiral routine for building the white-box demo.
- **Discipline over motivation (#5)** + **"no time" is a priority problem" (#6)** + **daily cadence**: this is the mechanism behind the coaching doc's weekly-planning ritual — a schedule you keep on bad days, not a mood.
- **"Compare only to yesterday" / don't put yourself in a position to compare (#8)** and the **negativity-bias / one-comment (#9)** ideas are aimed at the "prove them wrong" drive and the polarizing-feedback pattern: extract signal, decide yourself, don't let one voice set the project's worth.
- **Cut it in half / start small / the boring middle (#2–3)** and **corner-cutting is correct for prototypes (#28)** reinforce [[jonas-tyroller]]'s reduce-first and scrappy-prototype points — three-way agreement that the board game should be smaller than currently imagined and the demo code should be quick, not pristine.
- **The sunk-cost fallacy (#29)** is a live tool for the pivot itself: don't stay on a path (a role, a project scope, a study track) only because of what's already spent.
- **Team = marriage, expectations up front (#31)** applies the moment collaborators join after the loop works — complements [[tim-cain]]'s "manage each person individually" from the lead's side.
